03 / Before you land

Before You Land

Japan is easy to travel once you know a few unwritten rules. This guide focuses on the details that often surprise first-time visitors.

Your 10-minute setup

  • Keep some Japanese yen in cash. Cards are common, but cash is still useful.
  • Set up or buy a rechargeable IC transit card if it works for your route and device.
  • Save your hotel name and address in both English and Japanese.
  • Download an offline map area and a translation app.
  • Carry a small bag for trash; public bins can be limited.
  • Save emergency numbers: Police 110 / Fire & Ambulance 119.
New in November 2026

Tax-free shopping works differently for purchases from November 1, 2026. You now pay the full price first and get the tax back when you leave. See pages 5–6.

The five things most visitors should remember

  1. Tipping is generally not expected.
  2. Cash still matters.
  3. Trash bins may be hard to find.
  4. Trains are quiet spaces.
  5. “Sumimasen” is one of the most useful words you can learn.

04 / Money

Money Without Stress

Japan is much more cashless than it used to be, especially in major cities. Still, some smaller shops, rural businesses, ticket machines and local services may require cash.

A simple payment strategy

Card
Use it where accepted for hotels, larger shops and many restaurants.
Cash
Keep a backup amount for small businesses, rural areas and places that do not accept your card.
IC card
A prepaid transit card such as Suica, PASMO or ICOCA can make trains easier and can also be used at many vending machines, convenience stores and other locations.
Don't panic at the register

Look for card or IC logos near the register. If you are unsure, show your card and ask: “Kaado, daijoubu desu ka?” (Is card okay?)

Useful money phrase
Ikura desu ka?
How much is it?

05 / Tax-free shoppingNEW

Tax-Free Shopping: New Rules from Nov 1, 2026

Japan has changed how tax-free shopping works for visitors. For purchases from November 1, 2026, you pay the full price at the shop and the consumption tax is refunded later, when you leave Japan.

Until Oct 31, 2026

Tax was taken off at the register.

→
From Nov 1, 2026

Pay the full price, tax included. Get the tax back after Customs confirms at departure.

What to know

  • Minimum: 5,000 yen or more (before tax) at one tax-free shop on one day.
  • Leave within 90 days of the purchase date and do the check when you depart.
  • Food, drinks and cosmetics no longer need special sealed packaging.
  • The old 500,000 yen limit on consumables is gone, but buy only what you can personally carry out of Japan.
  • Items you use, eat or drink in Japan cannot be refunded.
  • Purchases made up to October 31, 2026 still follow the old system.
Ask when you pay

How the refund reaches you can depend on the shop or the refund service it uses. Ask at checkout whether any fee is deducted, and keep every receipt together with your passport.

06 / Tax-free shoppingNEW

At the Airport: The Tax-Free Check

The check happens at the airport or seaport where you leave Japan. Do it early.

Step by step

  1. Have your passport, all receipts and the items ready.
  2. Before you check in your bags, use a tax-free procedure terminal in the international departure lobby. At Narita, Haneda, Kansai, Chubu, Fukuoka, New Chitose and Naha you can also use Visit Japan Web. Checked bags cannot be taken back out.
  3. Green result: done, no inspection needed. Red result: take your items to the customs inspection area.
  4. Finish everything, including any inspection, before boarding.
One missing item = no refund for that receipt

Customs checks each receipt as one group. If even one item from a receipt is missing at departure, nothing on that receipt is refunded. If you plan to use something in Japan, it is safer to buy it on a separate receipt.

  • If you already used a tax-free item, don't use the terminal. Tell a customs officer at the customs counter.
  • Leave extra time. Neither Customs nor your airline compensates you for a missed flight.

Based on Japan Tourism Agency and JNTO information checked in October 2026. Steps may vary by airport; check official guidance before you shop.

07 / Trains

Trains Like a Local

Japan's rail system is extensive, but large stations can feel overwhelming. Focus on the line name, direction, platform and final destination shown on signs.

The easy version

  • Use an IC card for ordinary local travel where accepted: tap in, tap out.
  • Queue at the floor markings before the train arrives.
  • Let passengers exit before you board.
  • Keep phone calls off local trains and keep your voice low.
  • Move backpacks off your back on crowded trains.
  • Eating is fine on many long-distance trains such as the Shinkansen, but is generally avoided on local and subway trains.
Missed your stop?

Stay calm. Get off at the next station and use your map, or ask station staff. Staffed gates are your friend when a ticket or IC card does not work.

Station phrase
___ wa doko desu ka?
Where is ___?

08 / Manners

Small Manners, Big Difference

You do not need to memorize hundreds of rules. A few habits cover most situations.

No tipping by default
Tipping is generally not expected in restaurants, taxis and hotels. Pay the amount shown unless a service clearly says otherwise.
Shoes
Remove shoes when entering homes, many traditional accommodations, and some restaurants or temples. A raised entrance, shoe rack or slippers may mean a shoes-off area. Check signs or ask staff.
Queues
People line up for trains, restaurants and shops. Follow floor markings and join the end of the line.
Trash
Public trash bins can be limited. Carry a small bag and dispose of trash when you find an appropriate bin.
The golden rule

Try not to inconvenience the people around you. That one idea explains a lot of everyday etiquette in Japan.

09 / Convenience stores

Convenience Stores Are Your Friend

Japanese convenience stores, often called konbini, are useful travel bases, not just snack shops.

What they are useful for

  • Quick meals, drinks, snacks and basic toiletries.
  • ATMs at many locations; international card support varies by ATM and network.
  • Paying with cash, card or IC/e-money, depending on the store and terminal.
  • A reliable place to regroup, buy water and solve small travel problems.
Easy first meal

If ordering at a restaurant feels intimidating on day one, a konbini is a low-stress way to get familiar with Japanese food labels and prices.

Useful phrase
Kore, onegaishimasu.
This one, please.

10 / Restaurants

Restaurants Without Anxiety

Many restaurants display photos, plastic food models, ticket machines or tablet menus. You can often order with very little Japanese.

A simple routine

  • At the entrance, wait to be seated unless the setup clearly shows self-seating.
  • Use photos or point politely if language is difficult.
  • If there is a ticket machine, buy the meal ticket first and hand it to staff.
  • Do not leave a tip unless the place clearly works differently.
  • At busy places, avoid lingering long after finishing your meal.
Allergy note

Do not rely only on translation apps for serious food allergies. Prepare a clearly written allergy card in Japanese before your trip and confirm with staff.

Menu phrase
Eigo no menyuu wa arimasu ka?
Do you have an English menu?

11 / Situation guide · onsenNEW

Onsen (Hot Springs)

A hot spring bath is one of the best things to do in Japan. The rules are simple once you know the order.

How it usually works

  1. Change in the changing room. In most traditional baths, you bathe without a swimsuit in baths separated by gender.
  2. Wash and rinse your whole body at the shower stations before you get into the bath.
  3. Keep your small towel out of the water. Many people fold it on their head or leave it beside the bath.
  4. Tie up long hair so it does not touch the water.
  5. Dry off a little before going back into the changing room.
  • Avoid bathing right after drinking alcohol, and drink water.
  • Keep your voice low; many people come to relax.
Tattoos

Policies vary a lot. Some baths refuse tattoos, some let you cover a small one with a sticker, and some are tattoo-friendly. Check the facility's website or ask before you go. A private bath (kashikiri) can be an option.

Ask first
Tattoo wa daijoubu desu ka?
Are tattoos okay?

12 / Situation guide · toiletsNEW

Japanese Toilets

Public toilets are common in stations, parks and many shops. The buttons look complicated, but you only need a few.

Good to know

  • Toilet paper goes into the toilet. Put sanitary products and other items in the small bin.
  • The flush is often a button on a wall panel or side panel, a lever, or a sensor you wave your hand over.
  • Common labels: 流す = flush, 大 = big flush, 小 = small flush.
  • Do not press the call button (often marked 呼出) unless you need help.
  • Some toilets have a sound button that plays a flushing sound for privacy.
  • In homes, ryokan and some restaurants, there are separate toilet slippers. Take them off when you leave.
Can't find the flush?

Stand up and wait a second: many toilets flush automatically. If not, look at the wall beside you before touching the seat panel.

Most useful question
Toire wa doko desu ka?
Where is the restroom?

13 / Situation guide · taxis & luggageNEW

Taxis and Luggage

Taxis

  • The rear left door often opens and closes automatically. Let the driver handle it.
  • Show your destination on your phone, in Japanese if possible.
  • No tipping. Many taxis take cards or IC cards; look for stickers or ask.
  • Wear your seat belt, back seat included.
  • Taxi-hailing apps work in many big cities.

Luggage

  • Shinkansen (Tokaido, Sanyo, Kyushu, Nishi-Kyushu): a bag with total dimensions over 160 cm, up to 250 cm, needs a reserved “seat with an oversized baggage area”. Without one there is a 1,000 yen fee. Bags over 250 cm are not allowed. Strollers, sports equipment and musical instruments don't need this reservation.
  • Luggage delivery (takkyuubin) can send bags between hotels or to the airport. Ask your hotel front desk about cut-off times.
  • Station coin lockers are handy, but large ones fill up fast at busy times.
  • On crowded trains, hold your bag in front of you or use the rack.
Travel light between cities

Sending your big suitcase ahead and riding with a small day bag makes stations, stairs and crowded trains much easier.

14 / Situation guide · shrines & templesNEW

Shrines and Temples

Visitors are welcome at most shrines and temples. A few small gestures show respect.

At a shrine (Shinto)

  1. Bow once at the torii gate and walk slightly to the side, not through the exact center.
  2. At the water basin: hold the ladle in your right hand and rinse your left hand; switch and rinse your right; pour a little water into your left palm, rinse your mouth and spit beside the basin. Don't touch the ladle with your lips.
  3. At the front of most shrines: offer a coin, ring the bell if there is one, bow twice, clap twice, pray, then bow once more.

At a temple (Buddhist)

  • Pray quietly with your hands together. Don't clap.
  • Some temples offer incense; many visitors waft the smoke toward themselves.

Everywhere

  • Follow photo signs, keep your voice low, and step aside for people praying.
  • You may need to take off your shoes in some buildings.
Before taking photos
Shashin, daijoubu desu ka?
Is it okay to take photos?

15 / Situation guide · vending machines & escalatorsNEW

Vending Machines and Escalators

Vending machines

  • You will find them almost everywhere. Many take cash and IC cards; some take cards or QR payments.
  • Hot and cold drinks often share one machine: red labels usually mean hot, blue usually mean cold.
  • The bins next to machines are usually for bottles and cans only. Please don't put other trash in them.

Escalators

  • In the Tokyo area, people usually stand on the left; in the Osaka area, on the right.
  • More and more stations ask people to stand still on both sides and not walk. Saitama Prefecture and Nagoya City have local rules asking for this.
  • Hold the handrail. With a large suitcase, the elevator is often safer.
When in doubt

Copy the people in front of you. Local habits can differ from station to station.

17 / Mistakes

Common First-Timer Mistakes

Assuming everywhere takes cards.
Keep some cash as backup, especially outside major city centers.
Talking loudly on trains.
Keep conversations quiet and avoid phone calls on local trains.
Looking for a trash bin every five minutes.
Carry your trash until you find an appropriate place for it.
Tipping automatically.
In most everyday situations, it is unnecessary.
Trying to understand the whole station at once.
Only solve the next step: line, direction, platform, destination.
Checking in your bags before the tax-free check.
From Nov 1, 2026, do the tax-free procedure first. Your items need to be with you.
Overplanning every minute.
Leave buffer time for large stations, wrong exits and spontaneous discoveries.
Good traveler > perfect traveler

You will make small mistakes. Be polite, stay observant, and ask for help. That is enough.

When You Need Help

Save these before your trip.

Japan Visitor Hotline

JNTO operates a visitor hotline 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, with support in English, Chinese and Korean.

050-3816-2787

From a non-Japanese phone: +81-50-3816-2787. Check the official JNTO website for any updates before departure.

Lost something?

Ask station staff, the shop or the hotel first. Lost property is often handled by the facility where it was found before being transferred elsewhere.

Medical needs

For serious symptoms, use emergency services. For non-emergency care, ask your hotel or an official visitor support service for nearby options that can assist international travelers.

19 / First 24 hours

Your First 24 Hours

Use this as a low-stress landing plan.

At the airport

  • Get connected: eSIM, SIM or Wi‑Fi.
  • Get cash if needed.
  • Set up your IC card or confirm your train or bus ticket.

On the way to the hotel

  • Follow the route one transfer at a time.
  • Keep your hotel address ready in Japanese.

Near the hotel

  • Find the station entrance you will use tomorrow.
  • Locate a convenience store and an ATM option.
  • Buy water and a simple breakfast or snack.

Before sleeping

  • Check tomorrow's weather and first train route.
  • Charge your phone and power bank.
  • Put a small trash bag, cash and IC card in your day bag.
Day one goal

Do not try to conquer Japan on arrival day. Learn your neighborhood, eat something good, and get some sleep.
